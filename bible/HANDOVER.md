## Complete Creative + Production Handover
**Version:** 1 November 2026  
**Status:** Concept and reference development; no final continuous film verified  
**Primary creative lock:** THE TREE IS ALWAYS THE CAMERA  

> This is a complete handover, not a claim that the films or rig have been produced. “Locked” means agreed in conversation; “provisional” means a working suggestion.


## 1. Project at a glance

WORKING TITLE: LOOK WHO’S BARKING. Format: a collection of cinematic short-story vignettes seen entirely from one long-lived tree. Current development stories: The Lovers / The Mark and The Hanging. Handover state: creative direction and camera principle are established; reference stills exist, but neither film is a finished continuous video.

Creative premise: the tree is the witness, not a talking narrator. People live full lives in front of an unchanging point of view. The emotional effect comes from their ordinary, recognisable behaviour contrasted against the tree’s long-lived patience. Each story may choose a different attachment point on the same tree, but its camera origin stays fixed for that story.


### The 60-second brief for a new collaborator

- Never show an exterior “hero shot” of the tree in the finished story. The audience IS the tree; the camera looks outward from its trunk.
- Every story is perceived as one uninterrupted observation. The lens stays at one physical location; minor head-like left/right pans and up/down tilts are allowed. No camera travel, reverse shots or traditional coverage.
- The Lovers tells a warm, ultimately happy relationship across many years. Ordinary unhurried scenes alternate with smooth, genuine fast-forwarded life and seasons. The pregnant woman arrives alone at the end; her partner subsequently enters frame.
- The Hanging is a non-graphic scene: mob approaches, grandstanding speech, implied drop conveyed by sound/reactions, long hush, slow crowd dispersal. No graphic injury or visible hanging mechanism.
- Production preference has evolved: try a strict visual storyboard plus stable reference plates and AI video first. A Blender master environment is a technical fallback/consistency aid, NOT a prerequisite the user has agreed to build now.

## 2. Locked creative and camera rules


### Non-negotiable rules

- TREE POV ONLY. Camera is on or conceptually inside the tree’s trunk, viewing the world outward. We do not film the tree as an external subject within the finished vignette.
- SINGLE ORIGIN PER STORY. Lock camera XYZ for the entire story, including long timespans; another story may use another position on the same tree.
- ROTATION ONLY. Slow, small yaw/pitch adjustments are allowed as if a head could turn without the neck moving. No dolly, orbit, handheld translation, tracking, drone camera or reverse angle. Avoid zoom and lens changes.
- APPARENTLY CONTINUOUS FILM. No visible cuts, fades, wipes or jump transitions. Production can use hidden compositing joins, but the result should read as continuous observed time.
- NO DECORATIVE FOLIAGE FRAME. The latest approved perspective removes the conspicuous trunk at the edge AND the leaves vignette. Natural leaves may exist in the environment, but must not turn the composition into an “outside the tree” view.
- PHYSICAL MOTION AT FAST SPEED. Time ramps resemble smoothly accelerated playback of a real film. Travellers walk; a wagon rolls with turning wheels and moving horse; seasons and weather evolve. Not merely blur streaks, dissolves, or people teleporting.
- QUIET WORLD. Sparse rural activity, not a crowd every few minutes. The relationship supplies the central action. The Hanging is a deliberate exceptional crowd event.
- PERSPECTIVE DISCIPLINE. A shot can only show/hear what the chosen stationary tree mount plausibly would. A carved mark on its own bark cannot receive an exterior close-up.

## 3. Setting, geography and visual identity


### Location blueprint

- Rural, lightly travelled European-style countryside, loosely late nineteenth century (working era, not yet conclusively selected). Period dress and horse-drawn travel make the setting intentionally free of phones, cars and modern infrastructure.
- The tree sits off the country track in a grassy clearing. Earlier working separation was about 5–8 m from trunk to road. Treat as approximate and adjust only with conscious agreement.
- Foreground = grass and bare ground under the tree where people sit, meet, quarrel, kiss, carve and return. Midground = rutted track running diagonally, puddles, low wooden fence; background = meadows, wooded slopes and distant hills.
- Occasional recurring horse-drawn wagon/caravan passes one direction and returns in another season. Use recognisably the same wagon, route, driver or family where appropriate. A few farm animals and isolated travellers may appear.
- The road, fence, horizon and distant silhouette must not drift between generated clips; consistent geography is more important than decorative detail.

### Look and sound

Premium live-action photorealism, naturalistic performances and costume wear, detailed vegetation and clothing, restrained film texture, warm nostalgic autumn light as a key reference. Lighting should vary plausibly across days and seasons: the story cannot remain in perpetual golden hour. No illustrative fantasy sheen, overly glossy skin, exaggerated cinematic camera effects, or rapid music-video cutting.

Audio design is a major storytelling tool: wind, birds, cicadas, horse hooves, creaking wheels, seasonal rain, laughter, quiet dialogue, the scrape of the knife on bark, footsteps before the final reunion, and crowd sound in The Hanging. Sound remains continuous across time ramps; playback speed may be stylised while preserving recognisable natural transitions.


### Continuity reference versus artistic reference

The image with the orange circle explains the camera mount but is not a usable in-film shot. The early references with a huge tree trunk in frame establish colour and mood only. The later no-trunk, no-foliage image is the approved camera composition. Older technical boards may be consulted for story ideas but are not final approved blocking or geometry.

## 5. Story A: The Lovers / The Mark


### Dramatic intent and pacing

A slow, intimate relationship across many years rather than a short montage of isolated images. The audience watches two local teenagers play and gradually grow close, share an innocent first kiss, ordinary afternoons, an argument and reconciliation, carve their initials, and return later in adulthood. The ending initially suggests the woman is alone, then reveals a quiet, happy ongoing relationship when the man enters. No exposition is needed to explain a tragedy; the temporary uncertainty is simply a viewer assumption. A running time of roughly 6–10 minutes was discussed as a possibility, not approved as final.


### Continuous sequence / edit beats (NOT distinct camera shots)

- OPENING HOLD — Empty countryside. Introduce the stable view, road, ambient sound and tree scale. Let the quiet breathe.
- THE FIRST MEETING — Two teenagers enter the tree’s visible space. Boy sits with a knee drawn up; girl kneels or leans toward him near a wicker basket. Conversation, teasing, shared humour. No hurried exposition.
- EARLY FRIENDSHIP — They return or linger and muck around. Their movement feels unperformed and natural, including time spent doing nothing dramatically important.
- RAMP 1: THE WORLD CONTINUES — They leave. Smoothly accelerate real movement and seasonal changes. A wagon crosses the road, then weather cycles. Few other people appear.
- OLDER TEENS — Familiarity has become attraction. They talk in the same foreground location. Facial identity and scenery remain stable.
- FIRST KISS — A shy, innocent kiss, with hesitation, a laugh and space for a human reaction. No cut to a close-up.
- RAMP 2: MORE TIME — Days, rains, wagon journeys and perhaps a distant animal pass at sped-up playback speed; not a streaked time-lapse effect.
- YOUNG ADULTS — A long comfortable visit: sitting, chatting, leaning together, domestic plans or playful affection. Not every visit has a plot twist.
- ARGUMENT — A believable disagreement grows within the locked frame. One walks away; the other stays. No counter-shot or camera follow.
- RAMP 3: ABSENCE — Fast-forward the same clearing and recurring routines without either person present; the place persists.
- RECONCILIATION — The two return; an apology may be partly heard, but body language, hesitant touch and embrace do most of the work.
- THE CARVING — Their hand/knife approaches the tree-camera point or a plausible nearby portion of trunk. Scraping sound and their reactions convey carving initials; there is no external bark close-up.
- RAMP 4: YEARS OF ORDINARY LIFE — Repeat subtle background routines and seasons, with occasional uncomplicated visits. Character changes occur predominantly between absences, not through visible on-screen face morphing.
- SLOWDOWN — The scene gradually decelerates as the now pregnant adult woman comes into view alone. The timing invites a question but not a definitive implication of loss.
- HER QUIET MOMENT — She comes close to the tree and touches the old carving area. Wind, a few natural sounds, no wagon or crowd. Hold her mixed nostalgia and happiness.
- HIS RETURN — Footsteps, then the same man, now older, walks into the existing frame. They share a warm, undramatic reunion; perhaps a smile or hand on her stomach. A happy story, not a twist tragedy.
- FINAL HOLD — Both remain together quietly and may leave. Let the tree observe the settled clearing for a final breath without a cut or fade.

### The continuous-time grammar

Think of ordinary footage played faster, rather than a montage created from dissolves. At normal speed, scenes can last long enough for real conversation and silence. Between them, controlled speed ramps show complete animated actions and natural seasonal progressions. The camera stays stationary through speed changes. A character should leave the visible area before being regenerated older; avoid visibly transforming a teen into an adult in the same instant.


### Ending direction — locked

The pregnant woman does NOT necessarily mourn or imply that the man is dead. She visits alone, allowing the audience to temporarily interpret absence. He then enters the frame, revealing their relationship’s continuity. The surroundings stay still and undistracting. No wagon during this final reunion; no swelling reveal cut or sensational speech.


## 6. Story B: The Hanging


### Premise

A short, sombre historical event observed by the same tree from a different permissible mounting point. It is NOT a graphic execution scene. The power is in the approaching mob, political/religious grandstanding or public speech (exact rationale and words unwritten), changing crowd mood, an implied drop, then people dispersing as though the event were over. Do not add invented plot details to the screenplay until agreed.


### Continuous sequence

- THE EMPTY VIEW — Peaceful clearing and road. Crowd noise begins in distance.
- APPROACH — A local mob advances along the visible road and enters the tree’s clearing; bodies physically arrive rather than appear by cut.
- GATHERING — People assemble, shuffle and murmur; small human reactions and partial occlusion establish the crowd.
- GRANDSTANDING — A leader addresses the crowd in a sustained monologue. No close-up: blocking must make the speaker visible and audible from the fixed lens.
- THE CONDEMNED — The condemned man and low platform are part of this same geometry. Rope/branch mechanics are not displayed graphically.
- THE IMPLIED DROP — A wooden release/creak, sharp collective reaction and sudden hush. If necessary, the lower legs or torso enter a cropped edge of the view, but the face, neck, suspension point and injuries stay unseen.
- THE LONG HUSH — The gathering stands quietly. Some look away; small ambient sounds return.
- DISPERSAL — People depart slowly in ones and small groups down the track; the camera never follows or cuts away.
- AFTERMATH — The clearing returns to stillness. The tree watches. If the lower body remains partly visible, the composition must be non-graphic and emotionally restrained.

### Camera note

Older plans proposed a lower trunk anchor around 1.5–2.0 m to allow crowd/speaker and cropped lower body to share the field of view. This is a working proposal and may need geometric revision. The upper branch-junction marker is explicitly established for The Lovers, but the project permits a separate position for this different story. Any Hanging camera must still remain fixed throughout its own film.


## 7. Production path — strict storyboard first


### Current user preference

The user explicitly asked to explore making this without a preliminary 3D greybox/blockout, using very strict storyboards. Previous discussion recommended a Blender master scene for perfect continuity, and some earlier Blender rendering has been mentioned, but the production plan should NOT assume a completed master Blender scene exists. Treat a storyboard-first AI-video experiment as the chosen next test; adopt Blender if continuity errors prove too expensive to fix.


### Reference-only AI-first pipeline

- CREATE THE MASTER FRAME BIBLE. Adopt the approved elevated POV reference. Annotate camera origin, pan/tilt limits, road bend, fence posts, horizon, landscape contours, preferred crop, focal length estimate and the couple’s foreground blocking.
- LOCK IDENTITIES. Make frontal, 3/4 and profile references for each actor and age stage, plus outfit cards, hairstyle, colour palette and scale. Use the exact same reference file identifiers in every generation.
- MAKE A TRUE STORYBOARD. Instead of unrelated cinematic panels, derive all frames from one location composition. Show where characters are in that same world and specify any slight camera yaw/pitch. Each panel is a beat within an uninterrupted observation.
- MAKE A SEAM PLAN. Divide the continuous film into AI-generation segments according to action continuity. Include leading and trailing overlap with frame references, motion direction, sun/season state and audio. Choose seam locations when characters have left view, during prolonged simple motion, or amid a passing environmental event.
- GENERATE LOW-COST TESTS. Start with one 10–20-second normal-speed meeting interaction and one 10–20-second accelerated countryside interval, rather than attempting the whole film at once.
- REVIEW AGAINST THE FRAME BIBLE. Reject generation if tree appears on edge, lens height changes, road flips, fence drifts, faces change, body contact breaks, or wagon teleports.
- BUILD THE UNINTERRUPTED EDIT. Match outgoing and incoming frames, motion vectors, lighting and ambience. Composite/reproject/set-extend if necessary. Edit joins can be hidden in production; the audience should never perceive a camera cut.
- EXPAND ONLY AFTER ACCEPTANCE. Generate both actor-led scenes and interludes using consistent naming and the same reference archive. Keep an accepted take register; do not replace it with a new “similar” generation later.

### When Blender becomes useful

If AI-video generations repeatedly change perspective, road/fence topology, seasonal geography, or the relationship between actors and the tree, use Blender or another 3D scene as a fixed background, camera-calibration or mask/depth source. It can supply an anchor plate and animated background movement while AI supplies the expressive characters. This is a fallback/upgrade, not the currently mandatory first step.


### Why full-motion time ramps are challenging

Current clip-based generative video cannot be assumed to simulate years of evolving geometry and continuous actors perfectly. The project needs careful action animation, layered compositing, or many short controlled passes. A storyboard alone is not a guarantee of temporal continuity: it controls intention, while editorial/compositing controls the actual joins.


## 8. Storyboard and generation specification


### Mandatory card fields for every beat

- Beat ID and scene-time state (normal / accelerating / accelerated / decelerating).
- Single camera anchor ID. Lens/FOV/crop. Exact yaw and pitch relative to the master neutral direction. No change to XYZ.
- Ground-plane position of each person, wagon or animal relative to tree and road; entry/exit path and occlusions.
- Character identity/age/wardrobe IDs. Physical action at the start, during and end of each generated segment.
- Season, time of day, weather, tree-leaf state and sunlight direction; mark changes as continuous trajectories.
- Audio: dialogue lines if approved, ambience, movement, in/out sync, and offscreen cues.
- Reference asset names and revision; input image(s), segment duration, boundary-frame match, generator settings, acceptance/rejection notes.

### Canonical direction — image / video master prompt

“One photorealistic rural nineteenth-century country road observed from a camera embedded in the upper trunk of a large old oak approximately three to four metres above ground, looking outward and modestly downward into a grassy clearing. The observer IS the tree: do not show the tree trunk, do not construct a framing ring of foliage. Fixed physical camera location, fixed lens and stable landscape. Quiet country track running diagonally in the middle distance, weathered fence, meadows, wooded hills. Natural human-scale subjects in the foreground. Authentic period wardrobe, unforced subtle performances, convincing lighting, restrained cinema-realistic textures. This is a continuous observation with at most gentle head-like yaw/pitch; no cutaways, angle switches, camera travel, zooms or external shots of the tree.”


### Character instruction — teen meeting

“Use the approved Elias/Maeve teenage reference identities. Boy: dark curly hair, freckles, cream linen shirt, brown waistcoat, seated with one knee up; girl: distinct freckled face, copper-red braid, cream blouse, brown bodice, burgundy/rust skirt, leaning/kneeling beside a flower basket. Their interaction is shy, playful and natural. Maintain the camera viewpoint high on the tree. Their faces must remain independently recognisable from the reference sheet.”


### Time-passage instruction

“Accelerate the passage of actual, readable time in the same fixed landscape as if real uninterrupted movie footage were smoothly fast-forwarded: leaves change and fall, light travels, clouds move, rain passes, a horse pulls a wagon along the road with natural steps and turning wheels, rare travellers walk through and away. Maintain stable road geometry, fixed horizon and subject scale; no blur-only time travel, fade, montage cut or magically replacing objects.”


### Negative / reject prompt

“Do not place a giant tree trunk at the left or right edge. Do not show a tree from another camera. No foliage vignette, framing arch or decorative branches across the border. No aerial/drone/dolly/handheld motion. No reverse shots or close-ups. No invented fence posts, swapped road direction, new buildings or changing horizon. No re-styled faces, hair colour changes, extra fingers, warped fabric, impossible hands or time-lapse smear effects.”


## 9. Acceptance tests and quality gates


### Camera / visual continuity — reject if any fail

- Every accepted frame can be explained by one fixed physical origin on the selected tree and no visible camera travel.
- No tree trunk or foliage frame appears merely to “prove” tree POV; the POV is established by perspective and narrative rules.
- The road and fence are in the same world position in all time states. Sun shadows can change naturally; geography may not.
- Boy and girl keep distinct faces and correct hair colours; proportions, costumes, ground contact, basket and other props remain coherent.
- People, wagon and animals perform physically understandable motion. No teleporting, phase-through collisions or reversed direction.
- Accelerated sections look like actual footage played faster, not a collection of blurry still images.
- No visible splice, fade, abrupt lighting jump or inconsistent audio at segment joins.
- The final Lovers scene is calm, with woman then partner entering the same view; no irrelevant background wagon.
- The Hanging reveals no graphic injury, face/neck suspension point or external reaction close-up.

### Minimum viable proof

Produce two small tests, before buying enough credits for a full story: (A) a 10–20 second fixed elevated POV teen meeting with subtle motion and stable identity; (B) a 10–20 second real-action countryside speed-up with horse/wagon and weather changes. Then attempt an apparent single unbroken 30–45 second combined proof. Evaluate whether storyboard-only methods are viable, and introduce a 3D anchor only if needed. These durations are proposals, not locked runtime.
